Elementary Understanding / Two analytical experiments

Signals and receivers

A striking pattern still needs a source. A larger response still needs a noise budget.

Change one assumption at a time to see what an observation can establish. The first experiment creates a near-sidereal frequency from an ordinary daily signal. The second makes a passive receiver softer and follows the response, fluctuations and delay together.

Synthetic, analytically specified examples · no measured Saturn, geomagnetic or neural data. Research companion · unpromoted draft, not peer reviewed. Stable core: SPPT/ASTRA v1.0.7.

Follow the evidence, one link at a time

A recorded neural response, recoverable information, behavior and subjective experience are different outcomes. Evidence for one does not automatically establish the others. Each needs its own operational question and evidence; these mathematical examples settle none of them.

01 / Source attribution

Can an ordinary signal look cosmic?

Yes, in a precise and limited sense. Let a solar-day oscillation grow and shrink over a year. Multiplying those two variations creates a carrier and two neighboring frequencies, even though no sidereal source was put into the construction.

B(t) = [A₀ + A₁ cos(Ωyeart + ψ)] cos(ωsolart + φ)

Here A₀ = 1 arbitrary signal unit, φ = 0, and the adjustable modulation is m = A₁/A₀. The bound 0 ≤ m ≤ 1 keeps the envelope nonnegative. Phase ψ shifts the annual envelope without changing the three component amplitudes.

Why the upper sideband is near a sidereal day

In an idealized uniform prograde orbit, one extra rotation relative to distant stars occurs per orbital year compared with rotations relative to the Sun. Using a year of 365.25 mean solar days and a day of 86,400 seconds, 1 + 1/Y corresponds to about 86,164.1 seconds. That is the stated astronomical approximation, not an exact ephemeris or a treatment of orbital eccentricity, axial tilt and variable apparent solar time.

The mechanism follows directly from cos a cos b = ½[cos(a + b) + cos(a − b)]:

B(t) = A₀ cos(ωsolart + φ)
+ (A₁/2) cos[(ωsolar + Ωyear)t + φ + ψ]
+ (A₁/2) cos[(ωsolar − Ωyear)t + φ − ψ]

These equal sideband amplitudes follow from this simple sinusoidal amplitude-modulation model; they are not a universal diagnostic. Other modulation, mixtures and measurement windows can change what a spectrum looks like. Ordinary Fourier resolution of a finite record is of order 1 / record duration: separating year-spaced components generally calls for a record of order a year. This is not a universal limit on every model-based estimator.

A significant spectral peak alone does not identify its physical source. Significance answers a question about a specified statistical null, not which mechanism generated the signal. This counterexample does not establish an explanation for an unexamined dataset or a detected Saturn–INTERMAGNET relationship.

02 / Receiver sensitivity

More sensitive—or just noisier?

A softer restoring force lets the same input move a passive sensor farther. It also lets equilibrium thermal motion spread farther and makes the sensor settle more slowly. All three changes follow from one linear, overdamped model:

γ dθ/dt + κθ = μb(t) + ξ(t)
χ(ω) = μ / (κ + iγω)
variance = kBT / κ
relaxation time τ = γ / κ
cutoff frequency fc = κ / (2πγ)

θ is a small angular displacement, b the applied field, κ > 0 the stiffness, γ > 0 the damping, and μ the field-to-torque coupling. For an input proportional to eiωt, χ is the complex response; ω = 2πf. At the cutoff, the response magnitude is 1/√2 of its static value and its phase lag is 45°.

Assume a passive classical equilibrium bath at temperature T with zero-mean white thermal torque, ⟨ξ(t)ξ(t′)⟩ = 2kBTγ δ(t − t′). Inertia, active feedback, nonlinear saturation and added readout noise are omitted. The formulas apply within that model’s overdamped regime.

Soften the receiver and watch the tradeoff

Try stiffness 0.25 × reference. Static gain and relaxation time become four times larger; total thermal RMS becomes twice as large. Duration changes only the step-response window. Bandwidth changes only the integrated noise readout; neither control changes the receiver.

The input-equivalent floor stays the same

Sθ(f) = 4kBTγ / [κ² + (2πfγ)²]
Sb,eq(f) = Sθ(f) / |χ(2πf)|²
= 4kBTγ / μ²

0.00000166 T²/Hz; amplitude density 0.00129 T/√Hz

Dividing output thermal noise by the squared field response removes the stiffness dependence. Softening alone does not lower this model’s input-equivalent thermal noise floor when temperature, damping and coupling stay fixed. This is not a claim that every finite-time estimator or every signal-to-noise ratio is independent of stiffness. Different filters, readout noise, observation times or active mechanisms change the question.

Waiting and averaging are different choices

One relaxation time gives 63.2% of the eventual mean step response; three give about 95%. If the record ends earlier, static gain overstates what that step has achieved. Thermal samples separated by much less than τ are correlated, so collecting more closely spaced samples does not create equally many independent observations.

The duration slider only sets how long the step is observed. It does not silently impose a noise filter. A rectangular time average over a duration D has its own squared frequency weighting, [sin(πfD)/(πfD)]², in the noise integral; it is not a sharp cutoff at 1/D. The bandwidth control above instead specifies an ideal low-pass filter. This keeps spectral density, integrated fluctuations and observation duration distinct.

Units, assumptions and the biological boundary

These are invented teaching parameters: κ₀ = 10⁻¹⁸ J/rad², γ = 10⁻¹⁸ J·s/rad², μ = 10⁻¹⁶ J/(T·rad), T = 300 K and kB = 1.380649 × 10⁻²³ J/K. Radians are dimensionless in SI; angular labels are retained to show what is measured. The symbol T denotes temperature in the equations and tesla in field units. Every term in the motion equation has torque units J/rad. Gain has units rad/T, output PSD rad²/Hz and input-equivalent PSD T²/Hz.

The positive stiffness range excludes κ = 0, where this unconfined model has no stationary equilibrium variance. Full-band variance belongs to the ideal overdamped equation; a real receiver may need inertia and high-frequency corrections. The step plots show an ensemble mean, not measured motion or a simulated noise trace.

This conditional equilibrium model is not an identified human receptor or a demonstrated biological effect. Active biological systems require additional energy and noise modeling. Nothing here establishes a Saturn–consciousness connection, a new physical law, or a mechanism for subjective experience.
