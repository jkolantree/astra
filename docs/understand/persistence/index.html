Elementary Understanding / An optional connection

What persists, and what changes next?

A place can preserve a physical trace of its past. On the Moon, some cold sites retain molecules long enough to accumulate ice. In a neural circuit, a previous input can leave a bias that changes the next response. These are different mechanisms with a useful question in common: what is retained, where, for how long, and how does it affect what happens next?

The most promising connection to experience concerns temporary stability with the ability to change. An interpretation must last long enough to relate inputs across time, yet remain open to new evidence. A biologically specified model of that balance could help explain perceptual continuity and switching. ASTRA has not yet supplied such a uniquely successful model or explained why neural processing feels like anything.

A shadow can preserve a history

Shadow limits direct sunlight; sufficiently slow loss allows a particular substance to remain. Those are different conditions. A permanently shadowed region can still receive scattered sunlight and infrared heating, and it need not retain every volatile. The lunar far side is not permanently dark.

Local water detection in the LCROSS ejecta and near-infrared evidence of exposed polar ice establish physical material to investigate. Neither measures the complete global inventory of buried water.

A 2026 study reports a correlation between exposed-ice fraction inferred from ultraviolet observations and modeled permanent-shadow age. Its delivery, burial and loss model favors quasi-continuous accumulation over at least about 1.5 billion years, rather than one deposit-forming event. This is a published, model-conditioned historical inference; it does not identify every source or exclude individual impact contributions. Aharonson, Hayne and Schörghofer, 2026.

Small shadows add possible storage sites, but their thermal suitability is not proof that each contains ice. Trap lifetime, shadow volume, burial and escape toward warmer boundaries can change what survives. A colder site need not contain more ice if its delivery, age or other losses differ. Micro cold-trap study · trap creation and destruction model · 2026 lateral-loss model.

ASTRA’s proposed test is to account for those processes and predict a held-out crater, depth profile or later release. The age correlation is available evidence to build from; a model designed after reading it cannot call that same correlation an untouched test.

“Phase” and “trapping” name different things

Material retention
Molecules stay because escape is slow enough. A cold trap depends on the substance, temperature, supply, substrate and timescale. Physical memory means a surviving inventory or altered condition, not cognition.
Material phase partition
Water can occupy vapor, surface binding sites, ice or mineral-bound reservoirs. Their transfer and loss laws differ. Adsorbed molecules and a bulk ice deposit need not release material in proportion to inventory at the same rate.
Linear response lag
A reservoir can respond later than a periodic input simply because storage and release take time. No self-sustained oscillator or phase-locking mechanism is required.
Oscillator locking
Defined oscillations retain a bounded phase relationship over time. A spectral peak at the driving frequency can also come from repeated evoked responses; it is insufficient to establish locking.
Neural metastability
Neural organization can persist temporarily and then change. Noise-driven switches between attractors and deterministic transitions among transient patterns are different possible mechanisms. A basin of attraction is not a physical hole.
Experience
Seeing a colour, hearing a melody or feeling a thought occur concerns experience. A neural correlation, retained variable or synchrony score does not establish experience by definition.

The same scene can support a changing experience

An ambiguous image can remain unchanged while its perceived interpretation holds and then switches. Published work separates an attractive history effect, favoring the same interpretation again, from adaptation, which can favor a different interpretation. History can therefore support persistence and help end it. Perceptual-history study.

This gives a concrete target for a model: predict the next interpretation, how long it persists, and how these outcomes depend on prior inputs. Established stochastic competition and adaptation models already address such behavior. A new coupling-history account must improve predictions against these alternatives rather than rename their hidden states. Competing-integration model.

Coordination between neural regions may matter, but “more synchrony” is not a general measure of consciousness. For example, coherent frontal alpha activity can appear during propofol-associated loss of behavioral responsiveness. What interacts, at which frequencies and times, matters. Responsiveness is also an operational measure, not direct access to subjective experience. Primary neural-recording study.

A response can reveal what a snapshot misses

Wolff and colleagues found that EEG responses to a neutral visual impulse carried information about maintained memory items, including unattended items difficult to decode from ongoing activity. This illustrates the value of asking how a system responds. Wolff et al., 2017.

Barbosa and colleagues subsequently recovered ongoing memory information with more sensitive analyses and alpha-power measures, challenging the stronger claim that these memories were electrically silent. Their 2021 reanalysis must be read with its 2022 correction, which removed an invalid shuffle-predictor inset and revised the relevant results text.

The supported lesson is that different measurements expose different aspects of memory. The original decoding null does not prove exclusive storage in silent synapses. Conversely, finding memory-related information in a signal does not show that its content is currently experienced.

A proposed bridge: history changes what can interact next

The ambitious hypothesis is that some aspects of perceptual continuity and switching become more predictable when a model includes both active neural patterns and biologically specified changes in their coupling. Candidate history variables might represent measured adaptation or short-term synaptic processes. They need a content-specific encoding and readout mechanism; a scalar amount of stored “memory” does not explain what is remembered.

The critical comparison matches inputs and similar measured activity after different histories, then asks whether a measured history variable predicts later responses in new data. Similar recorded activity does not imply an identical complete biological state. Apparent memory can arise when the observer has omitted relevant state variables.

Three conjectural mechanisms make this more concrete. History-dependent short-term synaptic coupling might bias later reinstatement; Barbosa et al. (2020) supports parts of that account, not a complete mechanism of consciousness. A distribution of adaptation and recovery times might jointly predict decay, gain and phase, so a stable phase relationship need not imply oscillator locking. More speculatively, a retained coupling state might shift the boundary of genuine oscillator locking; that full causal chain remains untested.

The common proposed principle is write, survive, read: identify how a state is encoded, how it relaxes, and how a later process reads it. Those choices must make linked predictions on held-out data against ordinary adaptation models.

This is a proposed neural model comparison, not an established theory of consciousness. Lunar retention supplies a question about storage and inference. No evidence reviewed here links lunar cold traps or plasma to the generation or control of human experience.

Optional detail: persistence and lag can share one parameter

For a first-order reservoir with positive, constant release time τ, input c₀ + c₁ cos(ωt) and inventory M:

dM/dt = c₀ + c₁ cos(ωt) − M/τ
Steady mean: c₀τ
Oscillation amplitude: c₁τ / √[1 + (ωτ)²]
Phase lag: arctan(ωτ)
Initial differences decay as exp(−t/τ)

One timescale predicts decay, lag and attenuation together. A neural leaky integrator can share this mathematical form after its variables and units are defined. That would demonstrate ordinary filtering, not an ASTRA-specific result. The simple reservoir has no intrinsic oscillation when its drive is removed, no locking range and no multiple perceptual attractors. An input-output loop alone does not establish bistability or permanent memory.

The approximation has physical limits even on the Moon. Binding-site heterogeneity, diffusion and burial can require many timescales; fixed-area bulk-ice sublimation need not be proportional to remaining mass. Stored matter must appear once in an explicit inventory budget, rather than being counted again as an unexplained interface store.

A more substantive neural proposal has active variables x, biologically specified history variables m, input u and coupling K(m):

dx/dt = F(x, u, K(m)) + noise
dm/dt = G(x, m)
Recorded signal = H(x, m) + measurement noise

These are a research template, not validated equations for consciousness. A separate calibrated observation model must connect the states to perceptual reports and behavior. Changing an interaction’s strength does not automatically change the network’s adjacency.

What would count as progress?

For the Moon: fit declared delivery, thermal, transport and retention models on selected craters, then predict entirely held-out craters or geographic blocks and independent depth profiles. Compare strong existing heat-and-mass-transport models, not only a deliberately weak memoryless baseline. Demote an additional interface state if measured inventories suffice, its parameters cannot be identified, or its predictive gain disappears outside the training data. The 2026 lateral-loss model is a competitor, not a new ASTRA claim.

For perceptual history: use simulations and appropriately shared existing datasets to compare fixed coupling, conventional adaptation/noisy competition and specified history-dependent coupling. Match complexity or regularization, check parameter recovery, and hold out participants or sessions. Predict new interruption lengths, history orderings and switching statistics with the same fitted parameters. A retrospective fit with extra hidden variables is insufficient.

For oscillatory claims: distinguish endogenous locking from repeated evoked responses, common input, sensor mixing and filtering. Test predicted detuning behavior, phase slips and recovery in simulations or existing data. Matching a spectrum or dwell-time histogram alone does not distinguish the mechanisms.

For experience: separate neural information, attention, decisions, motor reports and responsiveness from conscious content. Existing no-report comparisons show why the reporting task itself needs modeling; their proxies also need validation. A model that predicts only button presses may explain decisions rather than experience. No-report rivalry study.

The meaningful advance would be one biologically specified model predicting several independent responses better than established alternatives, including where its effects should disappear. New human experimentation and clinical application are not proposed here.
